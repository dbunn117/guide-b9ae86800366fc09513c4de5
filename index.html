Start here

The first 60 seconds

  1. Check if I respond and breathe normally. Call my name and tap my shoulder. If I do not respond, call 911 on speaker.
  2. Not breathing normally, or only gasping? Start CPR and use an AED if one is available. Follow the dispatcher. Do not stop to look for my phone or kit.
  3. Unresponsive but breathing normally: roll me onto my side, mouth facing down so fluid can drain. Bend my top knee to keep me steady. If a neck or back injury is possible, ask the dispatcher before moving me.
  4. Unconscious, seizure, or too confused to swallow: assume my sugar is dangerously low. Put nothing in my mouth — no food, drink or gel; I could choke. Give glucagon from my bag (how: see the box below). Stay with me and watch my breathing.

How to give glucagon

Glucagon is a rescue medicine that raises blood sugar. It is not insulin. It cannot hurt me even if my sugar turns out not to be low, so if in doubt, give it. Read the label to see which kind I carry:

  • Nasal powder (Baqsimi) — a small tube. Don't test it first. Put the tip in one nostril until your fingers touch my nose, then push the plunger all the way in. I don't need to sniff or be awake.
  • Auto-injector pen (Gvoke HypoPen or Zegalogue) — on bare skin only, never through clothes. Pull off the cap, press the end firmly against my outer thigh, lower belly or upper arm, and hold: 5 seconds for Gvoke, 10 seconds for Zegalogue, until the window turns red.
  • Mixing kit (vial of powder plus syringe) — follow the picture steps inside the case: inject the liquid into the powder, swirl until clear, draw it back up, inject into my outer thigh or upper arm.

Then: keep me on my side — vomiting afterwards is common. I usually wake within about 15 minutes. If I don't and there is a second dose, give it with a new device. I still need emergency care after glucagon, even if I wake up. Once I'm awake and can swallow, give juice or glucose tablets.

Do not give me insulin. Do not delay emergency help to check my sugar, change a device, or call Jen.

Leave the pump (the white pod on my belly) where it is. Tell the paramedics I wear an insulin pump — they may remove it.

If I am awake and can swallow safely, check my sugar. If I seem low — shaky, sweaty, pale, confused, acting drunk — and you can't check, treat it as a low.

Before LOW or HIGH steps

How to check my sugar

If I can't wake, I'm having a seizure, or I can't swallow safely, go back to the first 60 seconds. Don't wait for a number.

1. Read my Dexcom G7 app

  1. Ask me to unlock my iPhone if I can. Open the app called Dexcom G7. The sensor on my upper arm sends readings to it automatically — you don't need to scan my arm.
  2. Read the number in the large circle, with an arrow beside it. The units are mg/dL. Use the current number, not an old point on the graph or an old alert.
  3. Check there is both a number and an arrow. If there's no number, it says the sensor is warming up, or the number doesn't match how I look, use the finger-prick meter below.

If the screen shows the word LOW instead of a number, my sugar is below 40 — that's dangerously low. The word HIGH means above 400 — very high.

The Omnipod 5 app shows the same sensor number on its home screen. Don't tap "Bolus" there — that button gives insulin.

Phone locked and I can't unlock it? Don't spend time on it. Use the meter in my bag instead.

2. Use the finger-prick meter if needed

In my bag: a glucose meter (small handheld screen), its glucose test strips, and lancets (tiny finger-pricking needles). Brands vary — the meter's own picture instructions win over these. Use strips labelled glucose, not ketone.

  1. Wash and dry my hands with soap and water. Sugar on my fingers gives a false high reading.
  2. Put a new, in-date glucose strip in the meter. Wait until it's ready for blood.
  3. Prick the side of my fingertip with a new lancet to get a small drop of blood.
  4. Touch the end of the strip to the drop. Read the number. If it shows an error, try again with a fresh strip — don't guess.
  5. Note the number and the time and tell the dispatcher or clinician. Put the used lancet in a hard container.

Can't work the meter? If I'm awake and seem low, don't wait — treat it as a low.

Treat now

LOW sugar — below 70 mg/dL

Signs: shaking, sweating, pale, hungry, weak, irritable, confused, slurred speech, acting drunk. I may insist I'm fine — check the number or treat me anyway.

Only give food or drink if I'm awake, can follow simple directions, and can swallow. Otherwise go to the first 60 seconds.

The Rule of 15

  1. Give 15 grams of fast sugar. Any one of these:
    • ½ cup (4 oz / 120 mL) of apple juice, or regular non-diet soda
    • Glucose tablets — usually 3 or 4; the label says how many make 15 g
    • 1 tablespoon of sugar or honey
  2. Wait 15 minutes. Stay with me. Set a timer on your own phone.
  3. Check my sugar again — the finger-prick meter is best if my symptoms and the sensor disagree. Still under 70? Give another 15 g and wait another 15 minutes.
  4. Once I'm 70 or above and rising: give me a snack with carbohydrate and protein, like a sandwich or crackers and cheese.

Call 911 if

I get worse, become too drowsy or confused to swallow, have a seizure, or I'm still under 70 after two rounds of sugar. After glucagon, I need emergency care even if I wake up.

Common mistakes

  • Don't give any insulin while treating a low — not even to "cover" the juice.
  • Diet, zero or sugar-free drinks do nothing. Check the label.
  • Chocolate, cookies and fatty food work too slowly for the first treatment.
  • Don't overdo it. 15 g, then wait. A whole bottle of juice sends me too high later.

Source: CDC: treating low blood sugar · Dexcom: symptoms that don't match readings

Get help

HIGH sugar & ketones

Very high sugar without enough insulin makes my body burn fat and produce acids called ketones. Too many ketones cause DKA (diabetic ketoacidosis), which is life-threatening and needs a hospital. On a pump it can build within hours if the pump stops delivering insulin.

Call 911 or go to the emergency room if

  • Blood ketones are 1.5 or higher, or a urine strip shows moderate or large
  • I'm vomiting and can't keep fluids down
  • I'm breathing fast and deep, or having trouble breathing
  • My breath smells fruity or like nail-polish remover
  • I have stomach pain, or I'm very drowsy or confused
  • My sugar stays at 300 mg/dL or above — still there when you recheck

Don't wait for both a high reading and a ketone result. Any one of these is enough.

What to do

  1. At 250 or above, or if I'm sick, check ketones if there's a test in my bag. Don't delay medical help to find it.
  2. Use a blood ketone meter — or my glucose meter only if its label or manual says it also reads ketone strips. The steps are the same as a sugar check, but with a strip labelled ketone. Urine ketone strips are a slower backup: dip, wait, match the color to the chart on the pot.
  3. Call Jen and get medical advice. If I'm awake and can swallow, give me water.

Is the pump working?

Tell the clinician I use an Omnipod 5 pump. If the pod on my belly is beeping continuously, has come loose or off, or the skin under it is wet or smells of insulin, I may be getting no insulin at all — say so.

Please don't give me insulin yourself — no correction dose, no injection. Keep my phone near me and follow the clinician's directions.

Routine help — only once I'm safe

These are not emergency steps. Only do them if I'm awake and can guide you, you've been shown how, or a clinician is talking you through it.

Help with a meal doseOnly if I'm alert and directing you

A "bolus" is a dose of insulin for food. Only do this if I'm alert, about to eat, and telling you what to do — or a clinician is guiding you. Never for someone who is confused or unconscious. If unsure, call Jen.

  1. On my unlocked iPhone, open Omnipod 5 → Bolus.
  2. Tap Carbs, enter the grams of carbohydrate in the meal, and tap Done. Never type grams into an insulin-units field. Never enter the juice used to treat a low.
  3. If the sensor number matches how I feel, tap Use Sensor. If it's missing or doubtful, stop and ask me or the clinician.
  4. Check the suggested dose with me or the clinician. Don't change the Total Bolus number.
  5. Tap Confirm, check the details, then START. Watch the progress on screen. If you're not sure it went through, don't repeat it — ask.

Don't change settings, switch modes, or add extra doses. If I go low, stop and treat the low.

Source: Insulet iPhone guide pp. 230, 234–238

Replace the G7 sensorNever urgent — use the meter meanwhile

A missing sensor doesn't stop my insulin. It only means using the finger-prick meter until a new one is ready.

  1. In Dexcom G7 → Connections → Sensor, choose Replace Sensor if ending one early. Peel off the old patch.
  2. Wash and dry your hands. Pick a new spot on the back of my upper arm, away from irritated skin and scars, and at least 3 inches from the pod. Clean it with alcohol and let it dry completely.
  3. Unscrew the applicator cap; don't touch inside. Press it firmly against the skin and press the button. Lift it away. Rub around the patch three times and press the sensor gently for 10 seconds. Add the overpatch.
  4. Follow the app: scan the code on the applicator from inside the Dexcom app, or type its 4-digit pairing code. Keep the applicator until both apps are connected.
  5. Warm-up: up to 30 minutes (60 if the box says G7 15 Day). No readings or alerts until it ends — use the meter.
  6. Connect it to Omnipod 5 too: Menu → Manage Sensor → ADD NEW → TAKE PHOTO, scan the applicator code, then CONFIRM. This can take up to 25 minutes. If it fails, get help.

Source: Dexcom: replace G7 · Dexcom: insertion · Dexcom: 15 Day warm-up · Insulet iPhone guide pp. 284–286

Replace the Omnipod podOnly with training or a clinician guiding you

Don't start unless you have a new pod, my insulin, and someone (me, Jen, or a clinician) to guide you to the end. Once the old pod is off, I get no insulin until the new one is running.

My insulin is Humalog, U-100, in a 10 mL vial. Never use a different strength.

  1. In the app: Menu → Pod → Change Pod → Deactivate. Then peel off the old pod and tap Set Up New Pod. Never wear two active pods.
  2. Wash your hands. Keep the new pod in its plastic tray. Using the syringe in the box, draw up at least 85 units of insulin (the MIN line), remove air bubbles, and push the needle straight down into the fill port on the pod. Empty the syringe. The pod beeps twice. Never fill a pod that's on me.
  3. Lay my iPhone touching the pod and tap NEXT. Wait until it says it's ready.
  4. Pick a spot on my belly at least 1 inch from the old site, 2 inches from my navel, and 3 inches from the sensor, ideally on the same side of my body. Clean with alcohol and let it dry.
  5. Remove the tab and the adhesive backing. Don't use a damaged pod, or one with the tiny tube already sticking out. Press it on firmly.
  6. Tap START — the pod inserts its own tiny tube (the "cannula") under my skin. If I'm slim, gently pinch the skin around the pod while it does. Check the window for the pink color and no leaking, and answer the app's questions.
  7. When prompted, return to Automated Mode. If anything is unclear, get help.

An alarm, leaking insulin, or a pod that fails: get help promptly, and check my sugar more often.
